## Why does interior colour matter on a storage bag?

Because most bags get opened in bad light. A duffle or transit bag gets
unzipped at a dark campsite, in a ute tray at dusk, or in a canopy with no
interior light of its own — and at that point, the colour of the inside of
the bag determines how easily you can actually see what's in it. A bag's
exterior colour has no bearing on this; it's purely about what happens
once the zip is open.

## What's the problem with a black interior specifically?

A black-on-black interior is a light trap. Black fabric absorbs most of
the light that reaches it rather than reflecting it back, so even a head
torch pointed directly into a black-lined bag produces poor contrast
between the lining and whatever's sitting inside it — especially anything
else dark-coloured, which describes a lot of camping gear. The practical
result, reported often enough to be a genuine pattern rather than an
edge case: digging through a black bag by feel because you can't actually
see the contents.

## What is bone, and why does it work better?

Bone is a warm, slightly warm-toned off-white — lighter and less clinical
than pure white, but light enough to do the actual job of a lining, which
is to reflect what light is available back toward your eyes rather than
absorb it. A bone lining is an interior fabric colour chosen for
reflectivity rather than for matching the bag's exterior. Against it, most
gear — including other dark-coloured gear — shows up in silhouette and
colour contrast the way it simply doesn't against black.

## How much difference does lining colour actually make?

| Interior colour | Light reflected back | Contrast against dark gear | Visible dirt marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black | Low — absorbs most incident light | Poor — dark gear disappears against it | Low |
| Bone | High — reflects available light | Good — most gear shows in silhouette and colour | Moderate |

The pattern holds regardless of light source — head torch, phone light or
ambient dusk light all behave the same way against a light versus a dark
surface, because it's a property of the fabric's reflectance, not the
light itself.

## Doesn't a light interior show dirt more?

A little, but that's a minor and mostly cosmetic trade-off against a
genuine usability gain. A bone interior will show grease or mud marks more
visibly than a black one would — but a bag lining is meant to be seen
into, not just kept looking clean, and the difference in how quickly you
can actually find something at night outweighs a lining that stays
pristine-looking. It's the same logic that puts light-coloured interiors
in torches, tackle boxes and tool cases: function over appearance, for a
surface whose whole job is visibility.

This isn't specific to bags — it's basic colour science. The
[International Commission on Illumination (CIE)](https://cie.co.at/),
the body that sets the technical standards lighting designers work to,
defines light surfaces as reflecting a large majority of incident light
while dark surfaces absorb most of it — which is the exact mechanism
behind why a bone interior is easier to see into than a black one under
the same light.

## Why isn't this standard on more gear?

It's hard to find a good technical reason — light lining fabric costs
about the same as dark lining fabric, and it doesn't add weight or
complexity. The most likely explanation is that most gear is designed to
look good on a shelf with the zip closed, where the exterior colour is all
that's visible, rather than designed around the moment the bag is actually
open and in use. That's the gap this addresses: matching the interior to
how the bag gets used, not to how it photographs closed.
