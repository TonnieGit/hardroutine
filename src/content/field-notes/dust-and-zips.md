## Why do zips fail so often on gear used in Australian dust?

Australian red dust is unusually fine — much of it sits in the silt and
clay particle range, smaller than beach sand — which means it packs into
the small gaps of a zip chain rather than just sitting on the surface.
Once that grit is lodged between the teeth, closing the zip grinds the
particles against the chain and the slider, wearing both down and
eventually seizing the mechanism. A zip that jams under load doesn't fail
quietly: forced past the jam, it either breaks the slider or tears the
fabric it's stitched to.

## What's the difference between zip chain types?

A zip chain is the row of interlocking teeth that a zip slider runs along
to open and close a fastening. Not all chains are built the same way, and
the differences matter directly for dust resistance.

| Chain type | Tooth material | Typical tooth gap | Dust resistance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coil (nylon spiral) | Moulded nylon coil | Small, tight | Low — packs with fine dust quickly |
| Fine metal | Stamped metal | Small | Low to moderate |
| VISLON (moulded plastic) | Injection-moulded acetal | Larger, more rounded | High — sheds grit rather than trapping it |

Coil zips are common because they're light, cheap and flexible, which
suits fashion and lightweight gear. They're a poor match for red dust
because their small, tightly spaced teeth are exactly the geometry that
traps fine particulate.

## Why does a coarser zip actually resist dust better?

It comes down to gap size and tooth shape. A coarse, moulded chain — the
kind commonly described by the trade name VISLON — has larger gaps
between teeth and a rounder, smoother tooth profile. Fine dust particles
that would lodge in a tight coil chain mostly fall through or brush off a
VISLON chain instead of packing into it. According to
[YKK's zipper technology documentation](https://www.ykk.com/), moulded
plastic chains in this style are specifically positioned for outdoor,
industrial and rugged-use applications where grit and repeated
opening under load are expected — which is a direct match for a bag
mounted on the back of a vehicle on unsealed roads.

## Does a storm flap actually make a difference?

Yes, and it addresses a separate failure mode from the chain itself. A
storm flap — a strip of fabric sewn over the zip — stops dust reaching the
chain in the first place, rather than just coping with dust once it's
there. The two measures compound: a coarse chain that clogs less, under a
flap that reduces how much grit reaches it at all, meaningfully outlasts
either measure on its own. This matters most on gear mounted externally
and exposed to airflow off the vehicle, which is exactly the environment a
rear wheel bag sits in.

## What should you check before buying dust-exposed gear?

Two things, specifically:

1. **Chain type** — look for a coarse, moulded chain (often marketed as
   VISLON or similar) rather than a fine coil or metal zip, on anything
   that'll see corrugated or dusty roads.
2. **Storm flap coverage** — a full-length flap over the chain, not a
   partial one that leaves the ends of the zip exposed.

This is the reasoning behind the zip specification on
[Hard Routine's rear wheel bags](/gear/rear-wheel-bags/) — the component
under the most sustained dust exposure on the vehicle gets the most
dust-resistant hardware available for it.
