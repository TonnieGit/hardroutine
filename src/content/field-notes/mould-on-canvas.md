## What is mould on canvas, and why does it grow there?

Mould is a fungus that digests organic material for food, and cotton
canvas — being a plant fibre — is food it can use. Synthetic fabrics like
coated nylon don't feed mould the same way, which is the core reason
canvas gear needs different handling. Mould needs three things to
establish: moisture, warmth, and time without disturbance. A damp swag or
tent rolled up and left packed supplies all three at once, which is why
mould on camping canvas is almost always a packing-and-storage problem,
not a fabric-quality one.

## How do you remove mould from canvas without damaging it?

The right method depends on how established the mould is. Treat lightly
first — you can always escalate, but you can't undo damage from a method
that was too aggressive for light mould.

| Severity | What it looks like | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Light bloom | Faint grey-green fuzz, no odour | Brush off dry outdoors, then sponge with a diluted white vinegar solution (1 part vinegar to 4 parts water) and air dry fully |
| Moderate | Visible spotting, musty smell | Soak in a diluted oxygen-bleach (sodium percarbonate) solution, scrub gently with a soft brush, rinse thoroughly, dry fully in the sun |
| Established | Dark staining, strong odour, spotting won't brush off | Extended oxygen-bleach soak, repeated as needed; accept that staining may be permanent even once the mould is dead |

Always brush off loose, dry mould outdoors before wetting the canvas —
wetting it first can push spores further into the weave. Never use
chlorine bleach on canvas: it breaks down the fibres and any
water-repellent coating faster than the mould does.

## Does mould weaken canvas, or is it just cosmetic?

Both, depending on how long it's been left. Early-stage mould is mostly
cosmetic — the fungus is feeding on the surface and on any residual soil
in the weave. Left long enough, it digests the cotton fibres themselves,
which measurably reduces tear strength. That's why the timing in
[how to pack up a wet swag without ruining it](/field-notes/packing-up-wet/)
matters beyond the smell: a swag mouldy for a weekend is a cleaning job, a
swag mouldy for a month may be a weaker one.

## How do you stop mould coming back after you've removed it?

This is the step most cleaning guides skip, because it's not a cleaning
step at all. Mould returns when canvas gets damp and stays packed, so
prevention is entirely about the gap between "wet" and "fully dry":

- **Dry canvas within 24–48 hours of it getting wet**, every time, not
  just when it's convenient.
- **Store canvas gear bone dry**, never rolled up "mostly dry" on the
  assumption it'll finish drying in the bag — it won't, and that's exactly
  the environment mould needs.
- **Use a vented storage bag**, not a sealed one, so any residual moisture
  has somewhere to go during transit or short-term storage.
- **Re-treat with a fabric-safe mould inhibitor** after a full clean and
  dry, if the gear lives somewhere consistently humid.

CSIRO's materials research on natural-fibre textiles is consistent on this
point: the controlling factor for fungal growth on cellulose fabrics is
moisture content and duration, not the fabric's initial quality. See
[CSIRO's published research](https://www.csiro.au/en/research) for the
broader science behind why drying time, not fabric grade, is what
determines outcomes here.

## Is it better to avoid canvas altogether?

Not necessarily — canvas breathes and wears well over years of use, which
matters for gear you sleep inside. But for a bag that only exists to
transport and store gear between trips, avoiding an organic fabric removes
the mould risk at the source rather than managing it. That's the reasoning
behind using coated nylon for [swag bags](/gear/swag-bags/): the swag
itself can stay canvas, but the sleeve it travels in doesn't need to be.
See [canvas or nylon: which is actually better for touring gear?](/field-notes/canvas-vs-nylon/)
for the fuller comparison.
