## What's the real difference between canvas and coated nylon?

Canvas is a woven cotton fabric — an organic fibre that breathes, absorbs
moisture, and can be repaired with a needle and thread. Coated nylon is a
synthetic woven fabric with a polyurethane coating that sheds water
instead of absorbing it, at roughly half the weight of an equivalent
canvas panel. The difference isn't quality — good canvas and good coated
nylon are both well-made fabrics. The difference is what each one does
when it gets wet, and that's what should decide which one belongs in a
given piece of gear.

## How much heavier is canvas than coated nylon?

Meaningfully heavier, both dry and especially once wet. A 70-litre canvas
duffle bag typically weighs upward of 2 kg empty; the same size in 500D
coated nylon oxford comes in around 1.1 kg. Wet, the gap widens further,
because canvas absorbs water into the fibre itself rather than shedding it
from the surface.

| Property | Cotton canvas | 500D PU-coated nylon |
|---|---|---|
| Typical 70L duffle weight (empty) | ~2.0–2.4 kg | ~1.1 kg |
| Water absorption | High — fibre absorbs water directly | Low — coating sheds surface water |
| Typical dry time (soaked, open air) | 24–48+ hours | Under 1 hour |
| UV resistance | Good, degrades slowly over years | Good, but coating breaks down faster in extreme heat |
| Breathability | High | Low to none |
| Repairability | Easy — sews and patches well | Harder — needs specific adhesives or welding |

## Why does canvas absorb water and nylon doesn't?

Cotton fibre is hydrophilic — it draws water into the fibre structure
itself, which is part of why canvas has been used for breathable outdoor
gear for well over a century. Coated nylon is woven from a synthetic
filament that doesn't absorb water on its own, and the polyurethane
coating on top seals the weave so water beads and runs off the surface
rather than soaking in. That's also why a canvas duffle left wet can gain
several kilos in absorbed water, while a coated nylon duffle mostly just
needs wiping down.

## Does that mean nylon is always the better choice?

No — it depends on what the bag is for. Breathability is a genuine
advantage of canvas where it matters, which is mainly gear you sleep
inside: a canvas swag manages the humidity of a body sleeping in it
overnight in a way a fully sealed synthetic shell can't. For a duffle bag
or a transit bag that just holds and moves other gear, that breathability
isn't doing useful work — it's just a path for water to get in. That's the
distinction behind
[why every Hard Routine product uses coated nylon rather than canvas](/the-fabric/):
the jobs these bags do don't need breathability, but they do need to
survive being wet, thrown in a ute tray, and packed away without drying
out properly.

## Which fabric holds up better to Australian UV?

Both hold up reasonably well, but for different reasons and with
different failure modes. Cotton canvas degrades slowly under UV over
years, losing tensile strength gradually. Coated nylon's fabric itself is
UV-stable, but the polyurethane coating can break down faster under
sustained extreme heat — which is a genuine trade-off worth knowing rather
than a reason to avoid the fabric. Either way, hardware — zips, webbing
and thread — typically fails before the base fabric does on both fabric
types, which is why UV-stabilised webbing and thread matter as much as
the shell fabric itself.

## So which should you actually buy?

If the bag is something you sleep in or wear against your body, canvas's
breathability is worth the weight. If the bag's only job is to carry and
protect other gear — a duffle, a transit bag, a gear tub replacement —
coated nylon wins on weight, water resistance and dry time, which are the
properties that actually matter for that job. See
