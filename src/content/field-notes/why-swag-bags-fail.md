## Why does a swag bag wear out before the swag does?

A swag bag is a single-use-shaped product doing a repeated-use job. It's
sewn to slide over a swag that's been rolled once, at the factory, dry, by
a machine. Every trip after that, a person rolls it — wet, in a hurry, at a
different angle each time. The bag never gets easier to use; it gets
harder, because canvas that's been slept in and rained on sits larger and
lumpier than it did on day one. The bag doesn't fail because the fabric
wears out. It fails because it was never sized for the thing it actually
has to hold.

## What are the three most common swag bag failures?

Across Australian 4WD and camping forums and product review sections, the
complaints cluster into three repeatable patterns, in order of how often
they're reported.

**Undersized for a real pack-up.** The single most common complaint is
that the bag fits the swag only when it's rolled tightly, dry, by someone
with time to spare. A swag rolled wet, at a campsite, in the rain, is
wider and lumpier — and the bag that measured fine in the showroom won't
close.

**Zip failure from grit and tension.** Fine-gauge zips — the kind sized to
save weight and cost — fill with sand and dust and stop sliding. Forced
past a jam, they catch the canvas of the swag itself, which is how a bag
failure becomes a torn tent.

**Mould from a sealed, unvented shell.** A waterproof bag with no vents
holds moisture in as effectively as it keeps rain out. A swag packed away
damp, inside a sealed PVC sleeve, is a closed, humid environment — the
exact conditions mould needs.

## What size swag bag do I actually need?

A swag bag needs to be sized for the swag at its largest real-world roll,
not its smallest. That means measuring the diameter of your swag rolled
loosely and slightly damp — approximating a rushed, wet pack-up — rather
than the dry, tight demo roll shown in a product photo. As a rule of
thumb, add no allowance for "getting better at rolling it": buy for the
bad day, not the good one.

| Swag type | Typical rolled diameter (dry, tight) | Realistic wet pack-up diameter |
|---|---|---|
| Single swag, thin mattress | 320–350 mm | 370–400 mm |
| Single swag, thick mattress | 350–380 mm | 400–450 mm |
| Double swag | 400–430 mm | 450–500 mm |
| King double swag | 450–480 mm | 500–550 mm |

## Why do zips fail faster on swag bags than on other gear?

Zips fail under two combined stresses that swag bags apply more than most
other gear: fine grit working into the chain, and side-load tension from a
bag that's slightly too full. A fine-gauge zip chain has smaller gaps
between teeth, so it packs with dust faster and needs more force to close
— and that force gets transferred sideways into the fabric next to the
zip, which is what tears. According to YKK, the manufacturer whose
[zip technology](https://www.ykk.com/) underpins most of the outdoor gear
industry, coarser vislon-type chains are specified for exactly this
combination of dust exposure and mechanical load, which is why heavier
duty bags use them over fine metal or nylon-coil zips.

## How do you stop a swag going mouldy in transit?

You give the moisture somewhere to go. A sealed bag with no airflow keeps
humidity trapped against the canvas indefinitely; a vented bag — mesh
panels positioned so driven rain can't reach them, usually under a storm
hood — lets water vapour escape while stopping liquid water getting in.
Venting doesn't dry a wet swag on its own. It buys time: the difference
between a swag that's still damp but stable after a two-day drive home,
and one that's been quietly growing mould in a sealed bag the entire way.

## What should you check before buying a replacement swag bag?

Four things, in priority order:

1. **Internal diameter and length**, measured against your swag's wet,
   loose roll — not its dry, tight one.
2. **Zip gauge** — a coarse, moulded chain (VISLON-type) rather than a
   fine metal or nylon-coil zip.
3. **Ventilation** — mesh panels under a storm hood, not a fully sealed
   shell.
4. **Webbing and carry handles** rated for the loaded weight of a wet
   swag, not an empty one.

A swag transit bag is the sleeve your swag lives in between trips, and
it's worth sizing and specifying separately from the swag itself rather
