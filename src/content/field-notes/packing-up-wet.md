## Can you pack a wet swag away without it going mouldy?

Yes, but only for a limited time. Packing a wet swag isn't the mistake —
leaving it packed is. A wet swag rolled up and left sealed in a bag for a
few hours or overnight during a drive home is normal and low-risk. The
same swag left rolled up for a week is a near-certain mould outcome. The
goal at camp isn't to avoid packing it wet — sometimes there's no
choice — it's to manage the clock from that point on.

## What's the right way to roll a wet swag?

Roll it looser than you would a dry one, and don't fight to make it small.
A tight roll traps water and air together against the canvas with no
escape path; a loose roll leaves gaps that let a little airflow continue
even while it's bagged. Before rolling:

1. Shake or brush off loose surface water and debris.
2. Fold rather than crush the mattress section, to avoid creasing water
   into the foam.
3. Roll from the foot end toward the head, loosely, accepting a larger
   finished diameter than your dry roll.
4. Use a bag sized for that larger, wetter roll — see
   [why swag bags fail, and what to look for in a replacement](/field-notes/why-swag-bags-fail/)
   for sizing guidance.

## Does it matter what bag I pack a wet swag into?

It matters more than almost anything else in this process. A sealed,
unvented bag holds humidity against the canvas with no way out, which
accelerates mould growth. A vented bag — mesh panels under a storm hood,
so rain can't track in while moisture can escape — slows the clock
significantly. Venting doesn't replace drying the swag properly. It
extends the window you have before you need to.

## How long can a wet swag stay packed before it goes mouldy?

There's no fixed number, because it depends on temperature, humidity and
how wet the swag actually is — but the pattern reported across Australian
camping forums is consistent enough to plan around.

| Time packed wet | Risk in a sealed bag | Risk in a vented bag |
|---|---|---|
| Under 24 hours | Low | Very low |
| 1–3 days | Moderate — visible mould possible | Low to moderate |
| 3–7 days | High — mould likely | Moderate |
| Over a week | Mould near-certain | High |

## What do you do with a wet swag once you're home?

Unroll it fully, in a dry, ventilated space, within 24 hours of arriving
home if at all possible. Full sun isn't necessary and can be harder on the
fabric's coatings over time — open shade with airflow is enough, provided
it's genuinely dry air. Don't pack it away again, even loosely, until it's
completely dry to the touch on both sides, including the underside where
it's sat against the ground.

The Bureau of Meteorology publishes national humidity and rainfall data
that makes the underlying risk obvious: as its
[climate data services](http://www.bom.gov.au/climate/data/) show, much of
coastal and northern Australia sits at humidity levels most of the year
that will support mould growth on organic fabric within days once it's
sealed damp — which is the actual mechanism this whole process is managing
around.

## What if you can't unpack the swag for several days?

If you're still travelling and can't fully unroll and dry the swag, at
least open the bag and loosen the roll at each stop to let air exchange
happen, rather than leaving it sealed for the entire trip. It's a
partial fix, not a substitute for proper drying, but it meaningfully slows
mould development compared to a swag left rolled tight in a sealed bag for
days on end. If mould does take hold despite this, see
[getting mould out of canvas — and stopping it coming back](/field-notes/mould-on-canvas/)
for removal methods by severity.

A vented [swag bag](/gear/swag-bags/) buys you the time this process
needs. It doesn't remove the need to actually dry the swag.
