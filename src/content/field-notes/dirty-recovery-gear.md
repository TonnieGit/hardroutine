## Why does recovery gear need different storage from the rest of your kit?

Because it's the gear most likely to come back from a trip wet, muddy and
under load-bearing stress, all at once. A snatch strap or set of shackles
used in an actual recovery has usually been dragged through mud, soaked,
and stretched near its rated capacity — any one of which is a reason to
handle it differently to a dry bag of clean clothes. Recovery gear is a
category of equipment used to extract a stuck vehicle, and by the nature
of that job it comes back from use in worse condition than almost anything
else in the vehicle.

## What's the actual failure risk of packing recovery gear away dirty?

Two separate risks, not one. First, mud and grit left in a strap's weave
work as an abrasive during the strap's next use, accelerating fibre wear
from the inside. Second — and less obvious — dried mud and dust can hide
surface damage, like abrasion or early fraying, that you'd otherwise spot
on a visual check before the next use. A strap that looks fine caked in
dry mud might not be fine underneath it. Neither risk shows up
immediately; both compound over several trips.

## How should you clean recovery gear before storing it?

1. **Rinse off loose mud and grit** with fresh water as soon as
   practical — dried-on mud is harder to fully remove and more abrasive in
   the meantime.
2. **Check webbing and stitching** while it's wet and mud is loosened,
   when damage is easiest to spot.
3. **Dry fully** before packing away — snatch straps and recovery tracks
   are usually synthetic (nylon or polyester webbing, polymer tracks) so
   they don't mildew the way canvas does, but trapped moisture against
   metal shackles and hooks causes corrosion.
4. **Store separately from clean gear** — even dried, recovery gear
   carries residual grit that transfers to anything packed against it.

## Does drying recovery gear matter as much as it does for canvas?

Less, but not zero. Most recovery gear — straps, tracks — is synthetic and
won't mould the way cotton canvas does. The reason to still dry it fully
is corrosion on metal components (shackles, hooks, winch hardware) and
because damp storage against other gear transfers moisture where it isn't
wanted. It's a different mechanism to the mould risk covered in
[getting mould out of canvas — and stopping it coming back](/field-notes/mould-on-canvas/),
but the same underlying principle: dry before you store, every time,
rather than when it's convenient.

## Why does separation matter — why not just throw it all in one bag?

Because mud, grit and the occasional sharp edge (D-shackle pins,
damaged track teeth) are exactly what you don't want in contact with
clean bedding, food or clothing. Keeping recovery gear, wet towels and
general mess in a dedicated space — separate from the cabin and separate
from clean camping gear — is the entire logic behind a rear wheel bag
mounted externally on the vehicle. See
[what goes in a rear wheel bag](/gear/rear-wheel-bags/#what-goes-in-a-rear-wheel-bag)
for the fuller list of what belongs in that category of storage.

## What should live in a dedicated dirty-gear bag?

Recovery straps and shackles after use, muddy recovery tracks, wet towels
and washing, firewood, and rubbish you're packing out. The common thread
is: wet, dirty, or liable to transfer mess onto anything it touches.

removable, hose-out liner specifically because this category of gear needs
to be cleaned out, not just packed away.
